#+TITLE: How a\\Timestamp\\Is Stored
#+SUBTITLE: A timestamp is three integers and no timezone. That explains why day zero is 1 March 2000, why the same timestamp prints differently on two machines, and why one can change under you.
#+AUTHOR: Micah Killian
#+DATE: 2026-10-12
#+TOPIC: Lisp
#+TYPE: Explanation
#+LIBRARY: local-time
#+VERIFIED: local-time 1.0.6, SBCL 2.6.3

* Three numbers
What is inside a timestamp? Three slots, each an integer:

| Slot   | Reader     | Holds                                              |
|--------+------------+----------------------------------------------------|
| =day=  | =day-of=   | Days since 1 March 2000. Negative before it.       |
| =sec=  | =sec-of=   | Seconds since midnight UTC on that day.            |
| =nsec= | =nsec-of=  | Nanoseconds into that second, 0 to 999,999,999.    |

#+BEGIN_SRC lisp
(let ((moment (local-time:parse-timestring "2026-10-12T08:30:00Z")))
  (list (local-time:day-of moment)
        (local-time:sec-of moment)
        (local-time:nsec-of moment)))
#+END_SRC

#+RESULTS:
(9721 30600 0)

Day 9721, and 30600 seconds into it: eight and a half hours. Year, month,
hour and weekday are not stored. They are worked out from these three each
time you ask.

The design is Erik Naggum's, from his paper /The Long, Painful History of
Time/, which local-time's manual credits for the split into days and
seconds and for the choice of day zero.

* Why days and seconds are apart
A single count of seconds, as Unix time is, would be simpler to store.
Keeping the day apart makes the common questions cheap. Whether two
moments are on the same day, how many days lie between them, what weekday
it is: each is arithmetic on =day= alone, with no division by 86400.

It also keeps the numbers small. The seconds never pass 86,400 and the
nanoseconds never pass a billion, so both are fixnums, and only the day
grows with the distance from 2000.

* Why day zero is 1 March 2000
#+BEGIN_SRC lisp
(list (local-time:day-of (local-time:parse-timestring "2000-03-01"))
      (local-time:day-of (local-time:parse-timestring "2000-02-29"))
      (local-time:day-of (local-time:parse-timestring "1970-01-01")))
#+END_SRC

#+RESULTS:
(0 -1 -11017)

The date looks arbitrary and is not. Turning a day number into a year and
a month means stepping over leap days, and a leap day in the middle of the
year makes every month after it depend on whether the year is a leap year.

Start the year on 1 March and the leap day is its last day. Then the
months have the same lengths in every year, and only the year's total
changes. local-time's table of month lengths is written in that order,
March first and February last: 31, 30, 31, 30, 31, 31, 30, 31, 30, 31, 31
and 28.

The year 2000 was chosen for the same reason one level up. The leap-year
rule repeats every 400 years, and 2000 is where a cycle turns over: the
day before day zero, 29 February 2000, is the leap day that only a year
divisible by 400 has. Counting from there, the 400-year, 100-year and
4-year cycles all start at zero.

Unix time counts from 1970, which is day -11017. =timestamp-to-unix= and
=unix-to-timestamp= convert with that one constant.

* No timezone inside
There is no fourth slot. A timestamp is a moment, the same one everywhere,
and "09:30 in Tokyo" is a way of writing it down.

So a timezone appears in only two places: reading text in, and writing
text out. Parsing uses the offset in the text to find the moment and then
forgets it:

#+BEGIN_SRC lisp
(local-time:timestamp=
 (local-time:parse-timestring "2026-12-25T09:00:00+09:00")
 (local-time:parse-timestring "2026-12-25T00:00:00Z"))
#+END_SRC

#+RESULTS:
T

Printing picks a zone, and when you don't name one it is
=*default-timezone*=, which starts as the zone of the machine. That is why
one timestamp prints two ways on two machines, and why a test that
compares printed timestamps passes on a laptop and fails on a server. The
timestamps are equal. Their printed forms are not.

It is also why the functions that take a timestamp apart, such as
=timestamp-hour= and =decode-timestamp=, accept =:timezone=. The hour is
not a property of the moment. It is a property of the moment and a place.

* The printed form hides some of it
The printed form shows six digits after the seconds. The slot holds nine:

#+BEGIN_SRC lisp
(let ((moment (local-time:parse-timestring "2026-12-25T09:30:00.123456789Z")))
  (list (prin1-to-string moment)
        (local-time:nsec-of moment)))
#+END_SRC

#+RESULTS:
("@2026-12-25T09:30:00.123456Z" 123456789)

Two timestamps can print alike and not be =timestamp==. Round-tripping a
timestamp through its printed form loses the last three digits.

* A timestamp can be changed
The three slots can be set. =day-of=, =sec-of= and =nsec-of= work with
=setf=, and =adjust-timestamp!= changes its argument, where
=adjust-timestamp= and =timestamp+= return a new one.

Most code never notices, because most functions return new timestamps. It
matters when two places hold the same object. local-time's own =today=
does this: it takes the timestamp the clock returns and zeroes its seconds
in place. A clock that keeps one timestamp and hands it out every time
will have it changed by the first call to =today=.

=clone-timestamp= makes a copy with the same three numbers.

* The rule
A timestamp is a count of days, seconds and nanoseconds from 1 March 2000
in UTC, and nothing else. Whenever a year, an hour, a weekday or a printed
string is involved, a timezone was used to make it, and if you didn't
choose one, =*default-timezone*= did.
