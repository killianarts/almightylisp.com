Cover Story · Macros

Bending the Language to Your Will

Macros are Lisp's most controversial gift — and the reason a patient writer can bend the whole language into a shape no committee ever anticipated.

There are no limits. A macro receives your code before it is run and hands back new code in its place, so the constructs you wish the language had can simply be written into existence — quietly, at compile time, with backquote and a comma or two.

That power is also the trouble. A macro that could have been a function is a small act of vandalism against the next reader. The craft, then, is knowing the difference: when to reach for one, when to let the evaluator do its ordinary work, and how to keep your expansions honest about the variables they capture.

This issue takes the whole tour — from a ten-line while to hygiene, variable capture, and the moment a masterful writer decides not to.

Learn the Language & the Editor at Once

Other books teach Common Lisp and then abandon you at the Emacs wall — a fine language on one side, an inscrutable editor on the other. This volume refuses the choice, and pulls the reader up and over the wall with both hands.

By setting the language and its editor in a single book, proficiency with each compounds rather than competes. You will be barfing and slurping while you C-c C-c your first macros, and you will not have noticed learning either.
