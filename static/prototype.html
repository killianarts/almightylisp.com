Suggested additions to essentials.org / the exported HTML

Cheap additions that make the chrome — and the book — more useful to the reader:

  • Section numbers in headings + anchors. publish.el already computes 3.8-style counters for the sidebar — print them in the heading itself. The § 3.8 section tab on the plates is then a link to its section (demonstrated in L and P), and readers can deep-link anywhere.
  • #+NAME: on demonstration blocks. One line buys a stable anchor — most useful on the blocks that demonstrate a form rather than define a function: #+NAME: unwind-protect-demo gives “as we saw with unwind-protect” somewhere concrete to point.
  • #+CAPTION: instructions on the “now do this” blocks (chip in L, banner in P): the chrome stops being decoration and becomes the task.
  • :description header where prose beats a symbol scan (L and P): DESCRIPTION: view commands for generating reports in the foot instead of the raw DEFINES list.
  • Copy button on shell blocks only (variant O). Lisp blocks stay type-it-yourself — typing is how the reader experiences Emacs. Terminal ceremony (brew install, doom install, running vend) is the one place copying is the right move, and :language already marks those blocks.

§ 9.5 ch.9 · errors & conditions

Preserving Your Stack

Being able to signal a condition, and then choose how to respond based on the condition is very nice, but Lisp has one more trick up its sleeve.

handler-case has a problem: it unwinds the stack. That means that you lose local state at the point where you decide to do something with a naughty-word condition. In the simple example above, it's difficult to understand what that looks like. To illustrate the problem with handler-case, we'll consider a different scenario: working with a stream of data.

Let's say we have a simple key=value style configuration system and a file like this:

We see a few errors. While parsing this file, we'd like to signal a condition and recover, report which lines are broken, what the broken lines look like, etc. and continue reading the rest of the file.

You don't have access to the skip-line restart, since that's established in the read-config loop. You've broken out of the loop and can't get back in. The stack has unwound.

That's where handler-bind and invoke-restart come to the rescue. Unlike handler-case, handler-bind can catch that malformed-line condition and then invoke the skip-line restart.

In this case, the debugger never popped up. Instead of manually choosing the restart for the broken lines, we used invoke-restart to skip them for us.

What if we try to call invoke-restart inside the handler-case version?

Won't work. The stack has been unwound, so we no longer have access to the skip-line restart. And you need restarts to be able to return to the loop iteration. If you call a normal function inside the handler-bind, the condition won't actually be handled. Instead, it will be "propagated" to the next outer call to handler-bind that also handles the same condition. invoke-restart is how you stop the propagation of the condition, handle the condition, and restart the process at the point where the condition was signaled.
