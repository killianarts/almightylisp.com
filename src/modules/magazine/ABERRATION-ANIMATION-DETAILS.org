#+title: The aberration animation
#+subtitle: How the magazine's screen effect is made in Chrome and in Safari, and why there are two

The magazine has one screen effect, in two sizes. Clicking an article link
or a book button plays the long one, *leaving*: the page splits into its
colour channels, tears apart, and switches off like a CRT. The next page
plays the short one, *arriving*: it starts split and settles. Arriving also
plays when the web fonts finish loading after the page has been drawn (see
=static/js/magazine-fonts.js=).

The effect is built two different ways. Chrome, and every other browser
that isn't Safari, puts the page through an SVG filter. Safari can't run that filter fast enough, so there
the page is copied, and the copies are tinted and moved instead. Everything
here is in =static/js/magazine-fx.js= and the "Click" section of
=static/css/magazine.css=, unless it says otherwise.

* Why there are two

The first version was the SVG filter alone, and it is still what Chrome
runs. In Safari it was unusable:

| Effect  | Safari, with the SVG filter | Safari, without it |
|---------+-----------------------------+--------------------|
| Leave   | about 2fps                  | about 55fps        |
| Arrive  | about 9fps                  | about 55fps        |
| Stalls  | up to 1.7s                  | none               |

(Safari at 1440x900 on a Retina screen. All the Safari numbers here are
from that setup, measured on 30 September 2026.)

The reason is where the filter runs. Chrome runs SVG filters on the GPU.
WebKit, the engine under Safari and under every browser on iOS, runs them
on the CPU, at full resolution, and runs them again whenever anything about
them changes. The effect changes the filter every frame, so Safari filters
the whole visible page, at twice its size on a Retina screen, sixty times a
second, on the CPU.

The filter is already limited to the part of the page on screen, and it
still has to run every frame. The only fast things in Safari are the ones
its compositor does without redrawing anything: moving, scaling and fading layers it has
already painted, and blending them. So Safari's version is built entirely
from those.

The script picks the path once, when it loads:

#+begin_src js
var SVG_FILTER = navigator.vendor !== "Apple Computer, Inc.";
if (!SVG_FILTER) root.classList.add("fx-lite");
#+end_src

=navigator.vendor= is "Apple Computer, Inc." in Safari and in every iOS
browser (they all have to use WebKit), and "Google Inc." or empty
elsewhere. Feature detection can't answer this one: Safari supports the
filter, it just can't run it quickly.

* What both versions share

** The timeline

Leaving takes =LEAVE_MS= (650ms) in three stages, set as fractions of that:

| Stage      | From  | To    | What happens                                        |
|------------+-------+-------+-----------------------------------------------------|
| Hit        | 0     | =HIT= (0.28)   | The full colour split, easing out (but not all the way) |
| Tear       | =HIT= | =BREAK= (0.71) | The page comes apart, getting worse                      |
| Switch-off | =BREAK= | 1   | The tear fades under the CRT switch-off              |

Arriving takes =ARRIVE_MS= (260ms): the split at full strength on the first
frame, easing out (=(1 - t)^3=).

Both stutter on purpose. During the hit, every third frame holds still (the
signal dropping out); during the tear, each broken state holds for two
frames (the signal catching). In Safari the stutter also hides some of the
cost of the frames that do change.

** The split

Three images of the page, red, green and blue, move apart. Red goes one
way and blue the other (=splitDir=, random per effect); green stays close
to the page (0.12 of red's distance). They part at an angle, not straight
sideways: =splitSlope= is the tangent of a random 15-35 degrees, up or
down, fixed for the effect, and each image's vertical offset follows its
horizontal one along it, plus a little jitter. Peak distance between red
and blue is =SPLIT= (30px).

Where the images overlap they add back up to the page, so black stays black
and white stays white. Where they don't, the colours show, and mix with
each other: on the light sheet, dark text gets cyan, yellow and magenta
fringes, with red, blue and green where two overlap; white text on the
black bands gets red, green and blue ones.

** The shake

The whole picture jumps sideways and skews (=JITTER= 14px, =SKEW= 1.5
degrees, scaled by the effect's strength), and in the tear jumps further
(40px), skews more (3 degrees), and now and then loses vertical hold (up to
28px up or down, a quarter of the time). In Chrome the sheet moves; in
Safari the copies do (=setShake=).

** The overlay (WebGL)

Over the page, both versions draw the same WebGL overlay: a canvas
(=.fx-canvas=, z-index 9001) with one fragment shader (=FRAG=). It draws:

- torn rows of red or cyan fringe;
- scanlines and grain;
- in the tear: blocks of static, bright seams, a rolling dark bar, and
  white flashes;
- leaving: the CRT switch-off, the picture collapsing to a bright
  horizontal line and then to the sheet colour.

The overlay runs fine everywhere, Safari included: it's one small draw
call a frame, at CSS-pixel resolution (=sizeCanvas= doesn't scale it up
for Retina, since bands and grain don't need it). It never sees the page:
browsers don't let a page draw its own HTML into a WebGL texture, which is
why the split and tear can't be done in the shader too.

** The rest

- During the tear, =html.fx-broken= turns the page background black, so
  torn-away parts show black.
- Magazine links are boosted by htmx: the next page is fetched while the
  effect plays and swapped in once the screen is off. Book buttons leave
  the site and do a normal page load after the effect.
- All the effect's elements hang off =<html>=, not =<body>=, so htmx never
  swaps them out or saves them in a history snapshot.

* Chrome: the SVG filter

The page goes through one SVG filter, =#fx-aberration=, built once
(=ensureFilter=) and applied to the sheet by =.sheet.fx-torn= in the CSS.
Each frame changes its attributes.

#+begin_example
feImage (a displacement map)            -> map
feDisplacementMap (page, map, scale)    -> torn
feColorMatrix (keep red)   -> feOffset (red's split offset)   -> rs
feColorMatrix (keep green) -> feOffset (green's split offset) -> gs
feColorMatrix (keep blue)  -> feOffset (blue's split offset)  -> bs
feBlend screen (rs, gs) -> feBlend screen (with bs)           -> the picture
#+end_example

1. *The tear.* =feDisplacementMap= moves each pixel of the page by the
   colour of the map at that point: red for sideways, green for up and
   down, 128 for nowhere. The map is a tiny canvas image (=feImage=,
   stretched over the window).
   - During the hit and while arriving, the maps are =makeStrips=: 2x96
     pixels of horizontal bands 1-9 pixels tall, 30% of them pushed a
     random distance sideways. A new one each frame, at a scale of up to
     =TEAR= (150px), makes rows of the page jump against each other. The
     map is stretched and smoothly interpolated, so the band edges come out
     soft, and the whole reads as a ripple.
   - During the tear, the maps are =makeTearMaps=: 160x120, each row left
     alone, thrown hundreds of pixels sideways, smeared into a streak (a
     gradient across the row), or slipped up or down in a block, at a scale
     of 300-800px. Pixels pulled from off screen come out empty, so the
     black background shows through.
2. *The split.* Three =feColorMatrix= primitives pull the torn page apart
   into its red, green and blue channels, three =feOffset= primitives move
   them apart (=setSplit= sets their =dx= and =dy=), and two =feBlend=
   primitives in =screen= mode add them back together.
3. *The region.* The filter covers only the part of the page on screen
   (=placeFilter=), not the whole article: filtering what can't be seen
   would cost more and show nothing.

The filter's rows each move independently, and that fine ripple is the one
thing Safari's version doesn't have.

* Safari: copies of the page

In Safari, =.fx-lite .sheet.fx-torn= turns the filter off, and the effect is
built from copies of the sheet, made when it starts (=copySheet=: a deep
clone, ids removed, =inert=, positioned exactly over the original). Each
copy is painted once, when it first appears, and after that only moved
with =transform=, which Safari's compositor does without redrawing.

** The split: three copies, added together

=buildSplit= makes a layer (=.fx-rgb=, fixed over the window, z-index
8999, black) with three copies of the sheet in it, one per channel
(=.fx-rgb-copy.r=, =.g=, =.b=). Each copy reaches =RGB_PAD= (80px) past the
window on every side, so the shake doesn't show its edges.

1. *Tint.* Each copy is tinted to one channel by an =::after= the size of
   the copy, filled with pure red, green or blue, in =mix-blend-mode:
   multiply=. Multiplying by pure red keeps the red channel and drops the
   other two.
2. *Add.* The copies are blended with =mix-blend-mode: plus-lighter=, which
   adds them, over the layer's black. Red plus green plus blue is the page
   again.
3. *Move.* =setSplit= stores each channel's offset, and =placeCopies= sets
   each copy's =transform= to its offset plus the shake. That is all a
   frame does.

This is the same arithmetic as the filter (separate the channels, offset
them, add them back), done by the compositor on layers it already has.
Chrome's filter moves the channels of one image; Safari moves three images
that each hold one channel.

Two things had to be right for it to work.

*** The primaries are Rec. 2020

Safari blends layers in the screen's own colour space, not in sRGB. On a
wide-gamut screen, sRGB's pure red isn't pure there: it has some green and
blue in it. So the first attempts, tinted with =rgb(255 0 0)= and so on,
didn't add back up to the page. Black came out maroon, the background came
out white, and the fringes came out lime, purple and brown. Display P3's
primaries were closer but still not pure on the screen tested.

Rec. 2020's primaries are outside any ordinary screen's gamut, so Safari
clips them to the screen's own pure primaries. Measured on the screen they
were tested on, =color(rec2020 1 0 0)= came out as exactly (255, 0, 0), and
the same for green and blue, at every brightness. So the tints are:

#+begin_src css
.fx-rgb-copy.r::after { background: rgb(255 0 0); background: color(rec2020 1 0 0); }
#+end_src

with sRGB as a fallback for browsers without =rec2020= (without it, the
copies would add up untinted and flash white). Lined up, the three copies
match the page to within a few levels.

This was only verified on one screen. Rec. 2020 should come out as pure
primaries on any screen, since every one is narrower, but other screens
haven't been measured.

*** Nothing in a copy gets its own layer

The tint has to blend with its own copy and nothing else. Safari's
compositor can't keep a blend inside a group of layers: if anything inside
a copy got a compositing layer of its own, the tint would mix with
everything under it, and the copies would come out wrong. So:

#+begin_src css
.fx-rgb-copy * {
    animation: none !important;
    transition: none !important;
    will-change: auto !important;
}
#+end_src

Each copy is painted as one flat image, tint included, and the compositor
only moves and adds those.

** The tear: copies in bands

=buildTear= makes a second layer (=.fx-tear=, z-index 9000, over the
split) for 12 bands (=TEAR_BANDS=). Each band is a strip of the window,
6-66px tall at a random height, holding its own copy of the sheet lined up
with the page. A band is =TEAR_PAD= (480px) wider than the window on each
side, and black past the page, so sliding it sideways opens a black gap,
as the filter's tear does.

=setBands= tears a share of the bands each update (35% during the hit, 55%
in the tear), sliding each up to the given reach (up to =TEAR_PAD=), and
now and then up or down. During the hit the bands change at most every
third frame (=liteDue=); in the tear, every update.

Three choices here came from measuring:

- *Bands that aren't tearing are moved off screen, not hidden.* Hiding and
  showing them with =opacity= or =clip-path= cost 40-60ms each time;
  moving costs next to nothing.
- *They're parked just past the window's edge* (=parkBand=), not far away.
  Parked far off, Safari threw away their painted pixels and painted them
  again every time they came back (45-58ms frames). Just past the edge, it
  keeps them (20-30ms).
- *They're added three a frame* (=growTear=, =TEAR_PER_FRAME=), over the
  first four frames, not all at once. Each band is a whole copy of the page
  for Safari to lay out, about 4ms each: twelve on the first frame, with the
  split's three, made it take 70-100ms.

The torn bands are plain copies of the page, not split ones, so torn
strips show the page in its own colours among the coloured fringes. Chrome
splits the torn page too. Splitting the bands would take three copies
each.

* How they look different

- *Chrome* has the ripple: during the hit, many thin rows of the page
  shift against each other, with soft edges. Its tear smears rows into
  streaks and slips blocks up and down.
- *Safari* has no ripple. Its split is the whole page moving at once, and
  its tear is 12 hard-edged strips sliding sideways over black.
- The split itself looks the same in both: same colours, same angle, the
  same mixing where colours overlap.

There's an argument that Safari's is the better look: cleaner, chunkier
breaks. Chrome could run it too (=plus-lighter= and =rec2020= work there),
but that hasn't been measured, and Chrome's filter is already smooth.

* Performance

Safari, 1440x900, Retina:

| Version                         | Leave     | Arrive    |
|---------------------------------+-----------+-----------|
| SVG filter (as Chrome)          | about 2fps | about 9fps |
| Text shadows (an earlier Safari version, below) | 28-31fps | 30-37fps |
| Copies, as now                  | 41-46fps  | 40-47fps  |

With the tear's bands hidden, the copies alone run at a steady 60fps after
the first frame. What's left to cost:

- *The first frames.* Building and painting the split's three copies takes
  about 50ms on the first frame, and painting them for the first time about
  70ms on the second.
- *New bands.* A band's first appearance costs a frame of up to about 60ms,
  once or twice per effect.
- *Band moves.* 20-30ms frames when several bands move at once.

* What was tried and set aside

In the order it was tried, for Safari.

- *Splitting the text, not the page.* Two =text-shadow= ghosts on every
  element, in the colours the filter would give on that background, and
  the text tinted. It ran at 45-53fps alone, but every change of the
  shadows made Safari redraw the text of the whole page (about 45ms), so
  the split could only change every third frame. Images got the same
  ghosts with =drop-shadow=. It was tuned a long way (an angle, separate
  palettes for the light sheet and the black bands, four and then five
  colours, Display P3 colours), but the colours couldn't overlap and mix,
  which is what the filter's look depends on. Replaced by the copies.
- *Tinting copies with =screen=.* Each copy screened with cyan, magenta or
  yellow, then the copies multiplied. It looked right for the first half
  second and then went wrong: the compositor can't keep a blend inside a
  group, so the tints mixed with everything under them. Replaced by
  multiply-then-add, with nothing composited inside a copy.
- *sRGB and Display P3 primaries.* Not pure in the screen's colour space;
  see above.
- *Building the copies before the click.* While the pointer rested on a
  link, the copies were built and kept lined up under the page, so the
  click would only have to move them. The first frames were no quicker
  (48-107ms), because the cost is Safari laying out and painting them when
  they appear, and the copies showed through the page. Removed.
- *The ripple.* A second set of three copies, shown through soft random
  stripes (a CSS mask) where the first set showed the rest, and slid
  sideways: Chrome's ripple, closely. Changing the mask each frame cost
  90ms a frame, so the stripes stayed the same and slid up and down
  instead, with the copies moved back by as much (other rows tear, the page
  stays put). That ran at 60fps once built, but building it cost about
  10fps over the effect: leave 30-36fps, arrive 22-40fps. Set aside for
  performance. Both versions are kept in =dev/fx-lab/= (below).

* Testing

=dev/fx-lab/= is a test page that plays the effect in a frame, in the live
version or a saved one, and times it frame by frame. It's outside
=static/=, so the site never serves it:

#+begin_src sh
python3 dev/fx-lab/serve.py   # with the dev server up on :5002
#+end_src

then open http://127.0.0.1:5090/fx-lab/. =serve.py= serves the test page
and passes everything else through to the dev server, so the test page and
the magazine pages in its frame share an origin. A saved version is
=dev/fx-lab/NAME.js=, a whole copy of =magazine-fx.js=, which the live
script loads in its own place for =?fx-lab=NAME= (only on localhost). The
two ripple versions are there: =ripple-staged= adds the second set a
colour a frame; =ripple-premask= has the stripes on from the first frame.
=hybrid= is Safari's version in every browser with Chrome's displacement
(no colour split) as an SVG filter over the split's copies: the ripple
and smeared tear on Safari's split. In Safari it costs what the filter
always costs there (leave 21fps, arrive 35fps, five runs each, against
51fps and 45fps for the live version).

The filter's displacement maps come in styles, in =distortions.js= (after
a set of monochrome glitch textures,
https://x.com/nexgridco/status/2105251311943893116): /classic/ (the live
maps), /blocks/ (datamosh: rectangles shifted or stretched as one), /shatter/
(wedges around a point pulled toward it, so they streak outward),
/smear/ (tracking: rows where one pixel's colour drags across) and
/liquid/ (rows shifted by layered sine waves, flowing frame to frame). The
=styles= version is the live effect with the style picked in the test
page's Distortion menu (=?fx-style=NAME=); =hybrid= uses it too. The live
script loads =distortions.js= ahead of any version it's asked for.

Path runs either version of the effect in any browser: Filter (Chrome's)
or Copies (Safari's), or Auto, the browser's own. It's =?fx-path=filter=
or =?fx-path=copies= on the page (only on localhost), which overrides the
=navigator.vendor= test. So Safari's filter stalls can be seen again in
Safari (an arrive at about 6fps, with a frame of over a second), and
Safari's copies tried in Chrome. The saved versions only differ in the
copies path.

Things learnt measuring in Safari:

- *Time it in the page.* Frame times from =requestAnimationFrame= show
  the stalls, frame by frame; that's what the test page records.
- *Safari stops drawing hidden windows.* If the display sleeps or the
  window is covered, =requestAnimationFrame= stops and screenshots go
  blank or stale. Check =document.visibilityState=.
- *Safari's screenshots are in the screen's colour space* (tagged with its
  ICC profile), not sRGB. Convert them before comparing colours.
- *Moving layers is cheap; anything that makes Safari repaint is not.* A
  version that measures 60fps should still be checked in a screenshot: some
  broken versions also ran at 60fps, because they weren't drawing anything.
